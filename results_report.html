Key Inferences

What the results tell us about the data, models, and physiological signals.

Random Forest is the Best Model Across All Metrics

RF achieves 75.9% accuracy vs SVM's 66.3%, XGBoost's 74.5%, and CNN's 52.3% — gaps that hold consistently across all 5 folds. The RF decision boundary, being non-linear and ensemble-based, captures complex physiological interactions without requiring spatial structure in the feature vector, giving it a fundamental advantage over all other models on this tabular input.

XGBoost is a Strong Second — Close to Random Forest

XGBoost achieves 74.5% accuracy and 0.676 F1 Macro, only 1.4 pp behind RF in accuracy and 2.5 pp in F1 Macro. XGBoost benefits from boosting (sequential error correction), balanced sample weights, and regularisation (reg_alpha, reg_lambda), making it more robust than SVM on this imbalanced dataset. Its per-class F1 scores (Distress=0.56, Relaxed=0.63, Mixed=0.85) are very close to RF's, making it a viable alternative when interpretability of individual trees is needed.

Skin Temperature is the Dominant Signal

Temp_Avg accounts for 22.3% of RF's total feature importance — more than double the next feature (PA_LED1_Clean at 10.1%). This aligns with physiology: skin temperature drops measurably under sympathetic nervous system activation (stress), making it a stable, low-noise discriminator across stress classes.

PPG Amplitude Outweighs Heart Rate

The top features are PPG amplitude channels (PA_LED1_Clean, PA_LED2_Clean, PA_LED1) and accelerometer standard deviations, collectively contributing ~44% of importance. Raw heart rate (HR_LED1) ranks last at 1.7%. This suggests peripheral blood flow dynamics and motion carry more stress-state information than heart rate alone for this sensor configuration.

Mixed Class is Easiest; Minority Classes are Harder

All models score highest on Mixed (RF/XGB F1=0.85, SVM=0.80, CNN=0.65) due to its majority-class dominance. Distress and Relaxed are consistently harder: RF peaks at 0.59 and 0.67 respectively; XGBoost closely follows at 0.56 and 0.63. The CNN's over-prediction of minority classes (Distress recall=0.57) at the expense of Mixed recall (0.48) reflects its aggressive class-balancing behaviour, which hurts overall F1 because Mixed dominates the dataset.

CNN Underperforms on Tabular Data — As Expected

The 1D CNN achieves 52.3% accuracy and 0.497 F1 Macro — below all three tree/kernel models. This is the expected outcome: convolutional filters learn local patterns across a "sequence," but the 16 physiological features here have no intrinsic ordering (adjacency is an artifact of engineering, not physics). CNNs need both spatial structure and thousands of samples to excel; neither condition holds here. With substantially more data and structured inputs (e.g. raw PPG time-series), this gap could narrow significantly.

No Eustress Data — 4-Class Model Not Yet Possible

Survey 7 (the only Eustress response) falls at t=10,576s, approximately 2,000 seconds after the sensor recording ended. Until a session is collected where the recording window overlaps with an Eustress survey window, the pipeline will produce 3-class models only. Adding Eustress will also allow genuine Mixed windows (surveys 5, 6, 8) to contribute feature data — and would give the CNN the scale it needs to be competitive.

Conclusions

Summary of findings and recommended next steps.

The pipeline is fully operational end-to-end with four classifiers: SVM, Random Forest, XGBoost, and a 1D CNN. Random Forest is the recommended model with 75.9% cross-validated accuracy and 0.701 F1 Macro on 3-class stress prediction from a single 2.4-hour wearable recording session. XGBoost is a close second at 74.5% accuracy and 0.676 F1 Macro — a viable alternative when gradient-boosted ensembles are preferred for interpretability or deployment size. Skin temperature, cleaned PPG amplitude, and accelerometer variance are the most discriminative physiological signals (from RF feature importances).

The 1D CNN (52.3% accuracy, 0.497 F1 Macro) ranks last on this dataset. This is consistent with established results on small tabular datasets: convolutional filters require spatial/sequential structure in the input and substantially more samples (~thousands per class) before they can compete with tree-based ensembles. The CNN's behaviour is not a failure of architecture or tuning — it is a data-volume and input-structure limitation.

The most impactful next step is data collection, not model tuning. Additional EMA sessions that extend the recording window into the later survey periods (17:16–17:35) will: (1) provide Eustress training data, (2) replace forward-filled Mixed labels with genuine Mixed-window observations, (3) increase Distress and Relaxed sample counts, and (4) give the CNN the scale it needs to be genuinely competitive.

Limitations

Known constraints that affect result interpretation.

  • Single participant, single session Results reflect one individual's physiology. Generalisation to other participants or conditions is unvalidated.
  • No Eustress class Survey 7 (Eustress) falls outside the recording window. The model cannot distinguish Eustress from other states.
  • Mixed label quality 519 of 861 rows are labeled Mixed via forward-fill from a survey answered ~1,170s after the recording ended — not from a directly observed Mixed window.
  • Severe class imbalance Mixed dominates at 60.3%. Despite balanced weighting, minority-class predictions are less reliable.
  • Small dataset for CV 861 rows across 5 folds gives ~172 test rows per fold. Standard deviations on per-fold metrics are correspondingly wide (±3–5 pp).
  • No held-out test set All data is used in CV. True generalisation performance on a fully unseen session is unknown until more data is collected.
